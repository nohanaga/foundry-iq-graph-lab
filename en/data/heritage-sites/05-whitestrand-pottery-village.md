# Whitestrand Pottery Village

> This is a fictional article. It is not a real World Heritage Site.

**Whitestrand Pottery Village** is a fictional pottery-making village on a coastal hillside in western Ireland. It is centred on a deposit of white clay, and its components are five bottle kilns, the clay pits, the slip houses and settling pits, the workshops, and the harbour from which the ware was shipped. Pottery has been made here from the late 18th century to the present day.

## Origins and history

White clay was known to outcrop on the hillside, and in the late 18th century potters built the first kiln and began to make ware. The clay fires to a clean white body and could be thrown thin, so its tableware sold well as an affordable everyday product.

The harbour lies on the coast about 2 km (about 1.2 miles) from the village. Finished ware was wrapped in straw, packed in wooden crates and carried by small boats out to larger ships anchored offshore. The harbour's stone quay and loading yard still stand.

## Bottle kilns and firing techniques

A bottle kiln is a tall brick structure shaped like a bottle. A domed oven, in which the ware is stacked, sits inside an outer conical shell that draws the fire. Fires burn in firemouths set around the base, and the heat rises through the oven. Each kiln has 7 to 9 firemouths, and the largest kiln is about 18 m (about 59 ft) tall and about 9 m (about 30 ft) across at its base.

A firing takes about three days, and the temperature is raised to around 1,250 °C (about 2,280 °F). Small spy holes in the walls let the firer check the colour of the flame inside. The firer watches the colour of the flame and the look of the smoke, and decides when to stoke each firemouth with coal.

## Workshops and village life

Each workshop gathers the rooms for preparing clay, throwing on the wheel and glazing under one roof. The clay is dug at the pits, mixed with water in the slip houses and passed through sieves into settling pits, where the fine particles sink and the coarse grit is left behind. Many workshops are run by families, with some members forming the ware and others tending the kilns. The airy drying lofts under the roofs let the ware on the racks dry evenly.

In the village lanes, fragments of misfired ware and broken saggars, the fireclay boxes in which ware is placed for firing, are set into the road surface and have long been used in garden walls and boundary walls. By reusing them, villagers created distinctive patterns in the lanes and walls.

## Conservation efforts

The bottle kilns are bound with iron bands to keep them from cracking, and the brickwork and firebrick linings are inspected regularly. One preserved kiln is fired twice a year. Each firing follows the method of the original builders, and the type and amount of coal are recorded.

At the clay pits, a quota for each plot limits how much clay may be dug, so that the deposit is not overworked. After digging, the soil is put back and the ground is planted with native vegetation. In this way, the shape of the hillside is kept, and the clay that future potters will need is left in the ground.

## Visitor information

At the village entrance, the Pottery Centre displays the whole process from forming to firing. Workshops can be visited only on open days, and wheel-throwing sessions must be booked in advance. Pieces made in a session are dried and fired, and they are posted to the visitor about one month later.

The path past the kilns is a sloping walk of about 600 m (about 0.4 mile) that runs along the sides of the kilns. From the top of the hill, you can see the whole village with its kiln stacks and the sea beyond. The bus from the nearest railway station takes about 25 minutes.
