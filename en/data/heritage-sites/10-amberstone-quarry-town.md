# Amberstone Quarry and Sandstone Town

> This is a fictional article. It is not a real World Heritage Site.

**Amberstone Quarry and Sandstone Town** is a fictional cultural heritage site in a valley in inland New South Wales, Australia. The components are the abandoned quarry, where a pale yellow sandstone called amberstone was cut, and the town streetscape of stores and houses built from that stone. The quarry covers about 18 hectares, and the streetscape runs along a street about 1.4 km long.

## Origins and history

Amberstone is a fine-grained sandstone formed from river sand laid down about 240 million years ago. It is easiest to work when freshly cut and hardens as it seasons in the air. From the 1840s, stores were built from the stone, and they spread as fire-resistant buildings, which were valuable in bushfire country.

In the late 19th century, demand for the stone grew across the colony, and the quarry reached its peak. The cut stone went not only to nearby towns but also to distant ports. After the 1960s, as concrete came into wide use, demand fell and quarrying shrank.

## Quarrying and haulage

Blocks were split from the rock face by cutting rows of small slots at regular intervals and driving wooden or iron wedges into them. The split blocks were then dressed into rectangular blocks of uniform size. The rows of slots remain on the quarry walls like a pattern, and they reveal the order in which the work was done.

For haulage, heavy blocks were dragged on timber sledges and carted on bullock drays down a haulage road laid at a gentle gradient, with logs set crosswise on the surface to ease the passage. The road leads down to the stone bridge at the foot of the slope. The bridge has three arches and can still be crossed on foot.

## The stone streetscape and daily life

About 120 sandstone buildings line the street. The masonry is laid in neat courses, and even within one property the stones of the foundation are larger than those of the walls. As the stone is exposed to the air, its surface gradually turns yellow and deepens in colour with the years.

Stone stores stay cool in summer and mild in winter, so they were used to keep flour, wine and wool. Some have now been converted into shops, galleries and cafes. On a corner of the street there is a working stonemason's workshop, where you can watch stone being dressed.

## Conservation efforts

Sandstone is vulnerable to water. Exposed to rain and wind, its surface wears away, and salts drawn up from the ground by damp crystallise in the stone and make it flake. When walls are repaired, damaged blocks are replaced with stone from the same quarry, and the original coursing is recorded in drawings. Wall surfaces are treated with a protective lime-based wash that lets the stone breathe.

The quarry walls are at risk of rockfall, so exclusion zones have been set up and sensors that measure the movement of the rock have been installed. To pass on the stonemasons' skills, the town's heritage society holds stonecutting workshops twice a year.

## Visitor information

The quarry can be walked on a loop path of about 1.5 km. The rows of slots in the walls and the large spaces from which the blocks were cut can be seen from viewing platforms with improved footing. In rain the rock is slippery, so the path is closed.

In the town, you walk along the street and view the stone buildings from the outside. The Stone Museum displays quarrying tools and models of the stone stores. From the railway station it is about 15 minutes on foot to the entrance of the street.
