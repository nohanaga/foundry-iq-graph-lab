# Fernbank Terraced Vineyards

> This is a fictional article. It is not a real World Heritage Site.

**Fernbank Terraced Vineyards** is a fictional cultural landscape in a valley in northern California, United States. It consists of about 1,800 vineyard terraces built on steep slopes, together with the irrigation channels and the village that support them. The slope drops about 300 m (about 980 ft) from top to bottom, and a system of channels connects the highest vineyard to the lowest.

## Origins and history

The first vineyard in the valley is said to have been planted in the 1860s on a gentle slope beside a spring. As the population grew, growers extended the vineyards onto steeper ground and stacked stone along the slope to make level benches. Because each terrace follows a contour line, every plot differs in size and in the curve of its wall.

In the 1880s, a new irrigation channel was built in the upper valley, and it extended the reach of the water supply. Village records of that project survive, and they show that each household had a fixed time and a fixed turn for drawing water.

## How the water is shared

Water flows from a storage pond at the head of the valley through the main channel and is divided terrace by terrace. Between plots, a small spillway cut into each wall lets water from an upper terrace drop onto the one below. When the flow is high, boards set across the channel block part of it so that it does not overflow.

Some sections of the channel cross ravines on wooden flumes. Each spring the flumes are inspected, and rotten boards are replaced with new redwood. The distribution of water is managed by a watermaster, a post filled in turn by the village households, who checks the flow at each terrace every morning.

## Village life and viticulture

Every household in the village takes part in the harvest, and pruning and planting are also shared work. Machinery cannot get onto the slopes, so the grapes are carried out in picking lugs, on shoulders, on hand carts and on small tracked carriers. Between tasks, workers eat lunch at the edge of the vines and talk over the condition of the neighboring plots.

The grapes from the terraces have small berries, and the wide gap between day and night temperatures is said to give them concentrated flavor with lively acidity. The village makes wine, grape juice, grape jam and red wine vinegar, which are sold at a farm stand. In winter, the main work is pruning the vines and repairing the stone walls.

## Conservation efforts

The walls are inspected every year, because the collapse of one wall affects the terraces below it. Master dry-stone masons carry out the repairs, and workshops held several times a year teach the craft to younger people. Only stone found in the valley is used, and it is laid to match the original pattern.

Where aging growers can no longer work their plots, a membership scheme lets people from cities in the region take over terraces. Members visit at pruning and harvest times and work under the guidance of the resident growers. Continued cultivation keeps the water flowing and the landscape intact.

## Visitor information

The best view of the whole landscape is from the overlook about halfway up the valley. In late winter, yellow wild mustard blooms between the rows. In summer the vines are green, and in autumn the leaves turn gold and crimson. Low evening light brings out the shadows of the walls, which attracts many photographers.

Visitors are limited to a dedicated trail that runs apart from the terraces. Walking on the walls or entering the vineyards is prohibited because it can cause collapse. The overlook is about 40 minutes by shuttle bus from the nearest bus station. Services are infrequent, so check the timetable in advance.
