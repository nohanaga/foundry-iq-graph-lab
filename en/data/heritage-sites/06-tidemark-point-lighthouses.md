# Tidemark Point Lighthouses

> This is a fictional article. It is not a real World Heritage Site.

**Tidemark Point Lighthouses** is a fictional maritime heritage site on a headland that juts into the sea on the Pembrokeshire coast of Wales, United Kingdom. It consists of three lighthouses and their ancillary buildings: the main lighthouse, the low light and the fog signal station, all built in the Victorian era, together with the cottages of the keepers who looked after them.

## Origins and history

Strong tidal streams and many reefs lie off the headland, and until the middle of the 19th century ships were wrecked there with grim regularity. In the 1850s, the lighthouse authority decided to build a light for the safety of shipping, and the first tower was raised on the summit of the headland. A consulting engineer designed it, and the stone for its foundations was hauled up from the shore by hand and by cart. The main tower was completed in 1858.

Later, ships grew larger, and on foggy days the light was hard to see. A fog signal station and a low light were therefore added below the headland, in 1889 and 1892, creating a system in which the three worked together. The lights were automated in 1989, and the keepers no longer lived on the headland.

## Lights and signals

The main lighthouse is built of stone, and its lantern at the top holds a large Fresnel lens. The lens is ground into a form that gathers the light of a small source into a beam that carries far out to sea, and it rotates so that the beam appears as a flash at regular intervals. The interval is different for each lighthouse, so a mariner can tell which light is in view.

The fog signal station housed a compressed-air device that sounded a low note in fog. Mariners judged the position of the headland from the direction and the interval of the sound. Inside the station, the signal apparatus and the air compressors survive in their original arrangement.

## Life of the lighthouse keepers

The keepers, who worked in shifts to tend the light through the night, lived at the station with their families. The cottages stand below the lighthouse, built with thick stone walls and slate roofs to resist the wind and the spray. The keepers' daily routine was lighting the lamp, cleaning the lens, taking weather observations and recording the ships that passed.

Each cottage garden had a windbreak wall and a small vegetable plot. The logbooks record the weather, the number of vessels that passed and the people who visited the headland. They are kept in order in the archive store and serve as a source on the sea conditions of the time.

## Conservation efforts

Because the site lies by the sea, salt damage to metal is the biggest problem. Doors and railings are repainted at regular intervals, and the brass and ironwork around the lens is cleaned of corrosion and coated with protective oil. The joints in the outer stonework are repointed with lime mortar so that salt water does not soak into the walls.

Special tools that do not scratch the glass are used to restore the lens. A repair record is kept for each part, together with photographs that show how the original component was fitted. To pass on the skills involved, some restoration work is open to visitors.

## Visitor information

The main lighthouse is open to visitors on weekends and bank holidays. About 110 steps spiral up to the observation gallery at the top, which looks out over the sea and the harbour. Visitors may not enter the lantern room, but they can stand right beside it. In strong winds the lighthouse is closed for safety.

The former keepers' cottages house a museum with lighthouse equipment, logbooks and charts. The path to the headland is about 1.2 km (about 0.75 mile) long and takes 25 minutes on foot from the car park. Waves can break over the rocks along the shore, so visitors are asked to stay on the maintained path.
