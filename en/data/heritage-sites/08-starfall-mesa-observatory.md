# Starfall Mesa Observatory

> This is a fictional article. It is not a real World Heritage Site.

**Starfall Mesa Observatory** is a fictional scientific heritage site on a high desert mesa in New Mexico, United States. It consists of an observing facility and the surrounding zone in which the night sky is kept dark. The observatory building, completed in 1912, and its 30 cm (12-inch) refracting telescope survive. The site lies at about 1,900 m (about 6,200 ft) above sea level, and there is no large city nearby.

## Origins and history

The site was first chosen as a weather observing station. Clear nights are frequent, the air is dry and star images stay steady, so it proved well suited to astronomy as well. In 1912, a group of university researchers built the observatory and imported the telescope from a European optical workshop.

The first observations were records of sunspots and measurements of the positions of comets. Observing expanded in the 1930s, when a large reflecting telescope was added. Research observing has since moved to a newer observatory, and the old observatory is preserved as a historical resource.

## The telescope and dome

The refractor gathers light with a lens, and its tube is about 4.5 m (about 15 ft) long. The heavy tube is carried on an equatorial mount that stands on a pier set into the ground, and a clockwork drive turns the telescope slowly to follow the rotation of the Earth. This lets the telescope keep a star in view for a long time.

The roof of the observatory is a hemispherical dome with a narrow slit. The slit was turned by hand to face the part of the sky being observed. The dome is painted white to limit heating by the sun and to protect the telescope from changes in temperature.

## Life of the observers

Observing is done at night, so the observers slept during the day and got up in the evening. The dining room of the lodge had a long table where they ate before going out to work. In winter, nighttime temperatures fell below -18 °C (0 °F), and the observers worked in layers of clothing.

The observing logbooks record the date, the time, the sky conditions and the names of the objects observed with great care. They also include pencil sketches of the surface of the Moon and the cloud bands of Jupiter as seen through the telescope. These records are used as source material for the history of astronomy.

## Conservation efforts

A dark night sky is one of the values of this observatory. The neighboring counties adopted an ordinance that requires outdoor lights to be fully shielded and aimed downward and to use bulbs with a low color temperature. Street lights are shielded so that no light escapes upward, and the lighting of signs at night is also restricted.

Once a year, specialist technicians remove the lens of the refractor and clean it. They use soft optical cloths so that the glass is not scratched. The rotating mechanism of the dome is lubricated regularly to keep it moving.

## Visitor information

During the day, visitors can tour the interior of the main building. A guide explains how the telescope works and demonstrates the opening of the dome. In the evening, star parties are held by reservation, and visitors can look at the Moon, planets and star clusters with commentary from the staff. Star parties take place only on clear nights and may be canceled because of the weather.

Because of the altitude, nights are cold even in summer, so you need to bring warm clothing. The observatory is about 50 minutes by shuttle bus from the nearest town. There is no bus back in the evening, so star-party guests either stay at a nearby inn or come by car.
