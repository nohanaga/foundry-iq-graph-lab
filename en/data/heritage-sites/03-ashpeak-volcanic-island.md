# Ashpeak Volcanic Island

> This is a fictional article. It is not a real World Heritage Site.

**Ashpeak Volcanic Island** is a natural heritage site on a fictional volcanic island off the east coast of New Zealand's North Island. The components are Mount Ashpeak, which rises from the middle of the island, and the lava plateau and sea cliffs around it. The island covers about 22 square kilometres, and its highest point is 840 m above sea level.

## Origins and history

The island is thought to have emerged when eruptions that began on the seafloor about 30,000 years ago built up above the sea surface. Eruptions have recurred several times, and the last major one took place in the late 19th century. Lava from that eruption formed a black plateau in the south-east of the island and forced part of a settlement to move.

Permanent settlement began in the 1830s, when fishing families built huts in a cove on the north-eastern side that was sheltered from the prevailing south-westerly winds. Islanders have kept records of the volcano's behaviour for generations. One diary notes that the water in a well grew warmer before an eruption.

## Lava plateau and vegetation succession

On the Blacksand Plateau, lava flows of different ages can be told apart at the surface. Shrubs grow on the older flows, while mosses and lichens are the first colonisers of the newer ones. Ferns follow the lichens, then mānuka scrub, and finally pōhutukawa forest on the oldest ground. By walking across the island you can follow the whole process, from bare rock to woodland.

The sea cliffs are lined with columnar joints that formed as the lava cooled. At the foot of the cliffs, waves have cut several caves where seabirds nest. In the breeding season, thousands of seabirds, including gannets and shearwaters, can be counted in the waters around the island.

## Island life and industry

The main industry is fishing. A warm ocean current from the north brings a wide variety of fish to the surrounding sea, and small boats that fish with hand lines leave the cove every morning. Soil mixed with volcanic ash drains well, so fields of kūmara (sweet potato) and avocado groves spread across the slopes.

Some greenhouses are heated by geothermal energy. Steam from beneath the ground keeps the soil warm, so seedlings can be raised even in winter. About 600 people live on the island, and small guesthouses for visitors stand around the cove.

## Conservation efforts

Seismometers, tiltmeters and gas sensors have been installed on Mount Ashpeak, and staff at the monitoring station watch the volcano around the clock. Islanders are informed of its activity through a five-level alert scale, and signs along the island's roads mark the evacuation routes.

Goats that were introduced long ago went feral and damaged the vegetation. They were captured over several years and are no longer on the island. To keep plant seeds out, there is a rule that soil must be cleaned from boots at the wharf before boarding the boat to the island.

## Visitor information

The island is about 2 hours from the mainland harbour by fast ferry. On the island, you can walk the track from the wharf to the crater rim or take the island bus. The return walk to the crater rim takes about 4 hours, and the track is closed if volcanic activity increases.

On the Blacksand Plateau, visitors must stay on the marked track, because stepping off it tramples the plants. The sea cliffs are best seen from the water on a cruise around the island. Cruises are cancelled in bad weather, so check the sailing status before you leave.
