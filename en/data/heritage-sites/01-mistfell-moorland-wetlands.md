# Mistfell Moorland Wetlands

> This is a fictional article. It is not a real World Heritage Site.

**Mistfell Moorland Wetlands** is a natural heritage site made up of three wetlands that spread across a fictional upland plateau in the Scottish Highlands, United Kingdom. The site includes Mistfell Flow, Bluelochan Bog and Peat Glen, and lies at elevations between 450 m (about 1,480 ft) and 700 m (about 2,300 ft). Mist forms over the plateau on most days of the year, and it gave the wetlands their name.

## Origins and history

About 11,000 years ago, as the last glaciers retreated, rain and meltwater began to collect in hollows that the ice had scoured out and lined with clay. Dead plant matter that stays under water decays only very slowly. Over the following millennia, sphagnum moss in particular piled up into a thick layer of peat, and the wetlands became blanket bog. Core sampling shows that the peat at Mistfell Flow reaches a depth of about 6 m (about 20 ft) at its thickest point.

In the 1930s, a plan was drawn up to cut drainage ditches so that the plateau could be turned into sheep pasture. Trial ditches caused the ground around them to subside, and the land proved useless as grazing. The plan was abandoned. The survey maps that recorded the trial are now kept at the Moorland Conservation Centre.

## Peat layers and water flow

The wetlands are fed by groundwater that seeps from the slopes of the plateau and by water that arrives as mist and rain. The peat holds water like a sponge, so the water table stays close to the surface all year round. In these conditions, water-tolerant sedges and sundews thrive, and because the peat contains so little oxygen, fallen leaves hardly decompose.

Bluelochan Bog has raised hummocks of peat, with narrow streams threading between them. The direction of flow shifts a little from season to season, and at snowmelt the water spreads over the hummocks. To capture these changes, water-level loggers and temperature loggers have been installed at several points.

## Local life and industry

In the villages at the foot of the plateau, people once cut rushes and heather on the grassland at the edge of the wetlands and used them to thatch roofs. The cutting took place in winter, when the ground froze, and the heavy bundles were hauled out on sledges. Few thatched houses remain today, but some villages continue the cutting as part of conservation work.

From spring to autumn, beekeepers make use of the flowers around the wetlands. Because the nectar plants bloom at different times, the hives are moved up and down the slope with the seasons, and in late summer they go up to the heather moor. Local beekeepers report how well the flowers are blooming to the Moorland Conservation Centre, and their notes form part of the observation record.

## Conservation efforts

Visitors are restricted to the boardwalks. The boardwalks are raised slightly above the ground, so they do not block the flow of water or compact the peat underfoot. Boot-cleaning stations stand at the entrances so that visitors do not carry in the seeds of non-native plants on their soles.

Where non-native plants are found, staff and volunteers pull them out by hand. The main target is seedlings of lodgepole pine, a conifer that seeds itself into open bog. In the old drainage ditches, small dams are being built to raise the groundwater back to its original level. Water-level data are compiled every year, and the progress of the recovery is published.

## Visitor information

The main attraction is the boardwalk loop of about 3.5 km (about 2.2 miles) around Mistfell Flow. It starts at the bus stop and car park on the plateau and takes about 90 minutes to walk. In early summer, white cotton-grass covers the bog, and on summer mornings you can see the landscape wrapped in mist.

Bluelochan Bog can be visited only on small guided walks that must be booked in advance. Footing is slippery on misty days, so waterproofs and non-slip footwear are recommended. In winter, snow closes the boardwalks, and only the exhibition room at the Moorland Conservation Centre stays open.
