# Cedarcrest Forest and Logging Railway

> This is a fictional article. It is not a real World Heritage Site.

**Cedarcrest Forest and Logging Railway** is a fictional mixed heritage site in a coastal mountain range of British Columbia, Canada. It preserves, as a single whole, a stand of old-growth western red cedar several centuries old and the remains of the logging railway that carried the cut timber out of the mountains. The forest covers about 3,200 hectares (about 7,900 acres), and the railway grade runs for about 38 km (about 24 miles).

## Origins and history

At Cedarcrest, heavy rain and a snowpack that lies for months have raised cedars that grow slowly. Slow growth produces tight, narrow growth rings, and the wood resists rot and splits cleanly, which made it prized for roofing shingles, siding and fence rails. In the early years, only a few selected trees were taken close to the rivers, and the logs were floated downstream. That method did not work in dry months, when the water was too low.

In 1912, a logging railway was built to bring timber out from deep in the mountains. It reached its peak in the 1940s and 1950s and was closed in 1967, when logging roads and trucks took over its work.

## Cedar forest and logging methods

The old-growth forest holds about 2,000 cedars more than 300 years old, and the largest has a trunk about 9 m (about 30 ft) in circumference. Moss grows thick at the base of the trees, and the forest floor is covered with seedlings and saplings that sprout from fallen trunks. Marbled murrelets, northern goshawks, northern flying squirrels and black bears live in the forest.

Loggers used selective cutting, in which only the mature trees are felled. The cut logs were hauled down the slope by steam donkey engines and overhead cable systems and gathered at the log pond. From the log pond, the railway ran about 38 km (about 24 miles) to the sawmill at the foot of the mountains.

## Railway engineering

The track gauge is 762 mm (2 ft 6 in), narrower than on ordinary railways, which suited the tight curves and steep grades. The line winds along the mountainside and has 14 tunnels and 22 bridges. The bridges are trestles of steel framing with timber decks, and some of them can still be walked.

The locomotives were small geared steam engines, and each pulled about ten log cars. On steep descents, brakemen applied the hand brakes on the cars to hold the speed down. The depot preserves two of the original locomotives and some rolling stock, and the tools used for repairs hang on the walls.

## Conservation efforts

Logging is forbidden in the old-growth forest, and access beyond the trails is restricted. Fallen trees are needed for the renewal of the forest, so they are left in place except where they block a trail. To avoid trampling the roots, visitors must keep to the designated trails.

On the railway grade, the main problems are rockfall at the tunnels and rot in the bridges. Bridge decking is replaced with cedar cut to the original dimensions. A survey of the grade is made once a year, and hazardous sections are closed to visitors.

## Visitor information

From the base station at the foot of the mountain, a restored section of the line about 4 km (about 2.5 miles) long is open to tourists, who ride in small rail cars. The cars make four round trips a day and pass through tunnels and across bridges along the way. A walking trail from the depot to the old-growth forest is about 2 km (about 1.2 miles) long and takes about 2 hours round trip.

In bad weather, the service is cancelled because of the risk of landslides. From winter to spring, snow closes the railway grade, and only the exhibition hall at the depot is open. From the nearest town, a local bus takes about 30 minutes to reach the base station.
