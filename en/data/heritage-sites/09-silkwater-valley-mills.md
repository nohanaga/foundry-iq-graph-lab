# Silkwater Valley Silk Mills

> This is a fictional article. It is not a real World Heritage Site.

**Silkwater Valley Silk Mills** is a fictional industrial heritage site made up of silk-making workshops and related facilities in a valley in New England, United States. The components are the sericulture farms, a cocoon drying house, a silk reeling mill, weaving workshops, a dye works, and the turnpike that carried the finished cloth to market.

## Origins and history

The valley has sunny slopes where mulberry trees grow well and a plentiful supply of clean water, so farmers took up sericulture there in the 1830s. Farms began it as a sideline, and the whole chain from cocoon to thread to cloth came to be completed inside the valley.

In the 1840s, a water-powered reeling mill was built in the valley, and thread began to be reeled by machine. In the 1910s, several workshops formed a guild, shared a pattern book and expanded their sales. After the 1960s, competition from imported silk grew stronger, and the number of workshops declined.

## Silk reeling and weaving techniques

At the drying house, the cocoons were heated to kill the pupae inside. They were then simmered in hot water to loosen the gum that holds the filament together and to find its end. Several filaments were combined into a single thread and wound onto a reel. In the reeling mill, a water wheel turned a train of gears that drove many reels at once, producing thread of an even thickness.

The looms are worked with foot treadles. A Jacquard mechanism raises and lowers the warp threads to suit the design, and the pattern is set by the positions of the holes punched in a chain of cards. About 40 looms that have been in use for more than 100 years are preserved in the workshops, and they can still be run.

## Life in the valley and dyeing

Dyeing was done with water from the river that runs through the valley. The dyers used indigo, madder, and dyes made from plants and bark, such as goldenrod and walnut hulls, together with wood-ash lye. Dyed skeins were hung on racks along the riverbank to dry, so the valley was lined with brightly colored thread. The stone vats of the dye works survive.

Most of the weavers were local families. During the midday break, neighbors gathered at the workshops to talk over patterns. Finished cloth was carried by wagon along the turnpike to the markets in town. Beside the turnpike, a toll house and the warehouses of the commission merchants who stored the goods still stand.

## Conservation efforts

The looms and tools receive an annual inspection and repair, focusing on the wooden parts, which wear easily. When a part breaks, it is replaced with a remade part of the same shape, and the original is kept with a record. The workshop buildings are checked regularly under the floors and in the attics to detect damage to the beams.

To pass on the skills of silk making, the local weavers' guild runs a training school. Students spend two years learning all the steps: making thread, dyeing and weaving. Some of the graduates settle in the valley and make products that reproduce traditional patterns.

## Visitor information

The Silk Museum occupies the former reeling mill and shows the steps of thread making in order. On a workshop tour, you can watch the weavers at work up close. Dyeing sessions are held by reservation, and you can take home a handkerchief dyed in the valley's water.

About 3 km (about 1.9 miles) of the turnpike, starting at the entrance to the valley, has been restored as a walking trail that passes the warehouses and the dye works. In spring the valley is bright with young mulberry leaves, and in autumn with fall foliage. The bus from the nearest railway station to the museum takes about 30 minutes.
