# Redcombe Copper Mine and Smelter Works

> This is a fictional article. It is not a real World Heritage Site.

**Redcombe Copper Mine and Smelter Works** is a fictional industrial heritage site in a steep-sided valley in South West England, United Kingdom. Copper mining began there in the early 18th century, was mechanised with steam power in the 19th century, and ended in the 1930s. The components are the adit and shafts, the engine houses, the ore dressing floors, the smelter chimney and the miners' village.

## Origins and history

In the early 18th century, a woodcutter gathering kindling noticed green-stained and rust-red ore exposed in the rock on the side of a stream. The first miners drove a hand-dug adit into the hillside, and the ore was carried down the valley in baskets on the backs of pack ponies. Iron staining from the rock face turned the stream red, which gave the combe, and later the mine, its name.

In the 1840s, a resident engineer introduced a deep shaft and steam beam engines to pump water out of the workings. Output rose several times over within a few years, and a smelter works was built in the valley. By the 1930s the lode had thinned and the mine could no longer pay its way, so it closed.

## Mine workings and ore dressing

The underground workings combined adits, which are horizontal tunnels driven into the hillside, with shafts that descend vertically. No. 2 Shaft is about 180 m (about 590 ft, or 98 fathoms) deep. Its engine house still stands, and the foundations of the headframe survive. Hand-drill and pick marks remain on the tunnel walls, and they show how the methods of working the rock changed over time.

The dressing floors step down the side of the valley. Ore was crushed on the upper level, and then water was used to separate the lighter waste rock from the heavier copper-bearing ore, which passed down to the next level. Water wheels and gravity did most of the work, which saved a great deal of hauling. Stone settling tanks and sections of timber launders, the wooden troughs that carried the water, survive as remains.

## Life in the mining village

At its peak, about 3,000 people lived in the valley. Rows of cottages, a company store, a public washhouse, a reading room and a school lined the hillside, and a tramway ran through the village from the workings. The cottages were built to the same plan, and each terrace was told apart by the colour of its doors.

After the mine closed, most residents left the valley, but some of the cottages continued to be lived in. The former village school became the Mining Museum, where miners' lamps, surveying instruments, wage books and pay packets are on display.

## Conservation efforts

The tunnels are prone to collapse, so the rock along the visitor route is inspected regularly. Roof repairs use the same type of timber as the surviving props and are carried out in a way that keeps the original form visible. The brick smelter chimney, about 32 m (about 105 ft) tall, is fitted with crack gauges so that any change can be monitored.

Water seeping out of the workings contains dissolved metals, so it is treated at a water treatment plant before it is released into the river. The water quality is tested every month, and the results are published. Some of the disused workings serve as hibernation roosts for horseshoe bats, and visits are restricted there to protect the habitat.

## Visitor information

Tours begin at the Mining Museum. Visitors put on hard hats and walk about 400 m (about 1,300 ft) of tunnel with a guide. The air in the tunnel stays at about 12 °C (about 54 °F) all year, which feels cool in summer and mild in winter. The dressing floors are reached by a walk of about 20 minutes along a path beside the stream.

From the nearest railway station, the bus to the museum takes about 35 minutes. Underground tours run six times a day, and booking is required. In winter, when the valley path can be icy or waterlogged, only the underground tours operate, and the path to the dressing floors is closed.
